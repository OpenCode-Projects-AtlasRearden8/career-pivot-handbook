# Chapter 4: Portfolio-First Proof

Here's the pivot's unfair advantage, sitting in plain sight: **nobody can
verify your "ten years of experience," but anyone can click a link.**

A portfolio is proof. A résumé is a claim. When you don't have the standard
background, proof beats claims — always has, on every hiring committee.

## What counts as "the proof" (larger than you think)

You don't need paid-by-a-client work. In Maya's world, these all count:

- **Explainers.** A 700-word plain-English piece: "What is an AI chatbot,
  really? (For people with jobs that leave them no time)." — published on her
  free GitHub Pages site.
- **Redesigns.** The hospital's patient-discharge
  instructions, rewritten by her for clarity, with a short "why" note — using
  an entirely invented example visit, **never real
  invented/anonymized**, obviously, never real procedure content.
- **Templates.** A fill-in worksheet ("First-30-days AI for busy nurses") —
  downloadable, MIT-licensed, free.
- **Public writing.** Four replies in an open forum where she explained AI
  versions simply; screenshots as examples.
- **Small artifacts.** A tiny spreadsheet template that turns nurse-meeting
  notes into cleaned topics — boring to build, *extremely* convincing.

## The "credibility" site, not the "pretty" site

Portfolio sites die of vanity: custom fonts, "coming soon" pages. Yours needs
exactly three pages, fast and readable:

1. **Home** — 5 lines: who you're becoming, who you help, what's here.
2. **Work** — the pieces, each with a two-sentence setup (the problem) and a
   link. Old, small, imperfect pieces are fine. They're *proof of reps*.
3. **About/Contact** — your honest story in plain words + an email you're
   willing to check.

Free hosting, zero code required: **GitHub Pages** (free) — or, if you'd
rather not make an account, this handbook's own `build.py` produces the exact
same style of site, and you can eyeball it locally and print it to PDF when
you need to show a human.

## The publication ladder (Maya's path)

1. Post in a private space (Discord, forum) — low stakes.
2. Guest piece in a niche newsletter — medium stakes, friendly editor.
3. Medium or Substack or a dev-community post — public.
4. A cold-but-specific pitch to a steady publication: *your* angle ("I will
   write about frontline nurses and AI adoption, first-person, 800 words").

Each rung is scary. Each rung gets easier. Nobody remembers your rung three;
they only see rung four.

## How the AI helps here (and where it stops)

- **Idea help:** ask for 15 angles on "AI for frontline healthcare workers."
  Reject 12. Keep 3. Write those yourself, first-person, nurse-voice: that's
  your moat — your authenticity, not formulaic AI paragraphs.
- **Edit help:** after your full draft, paste it and ask *"Show only the top
  three places a reader would lose interest."* Edit those, ignore the rest.
- **Where it stops:** the voice. If you paste AI-parrot filler into
  portfolio pieces, reviewers can smell it, and you lose your whole
  differentiated advantage. Keep your rough edges; they're *your edges*.
