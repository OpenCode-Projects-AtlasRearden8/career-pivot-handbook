# Chapter 7: Impostor Feelings (Pep Talks)

Every pivot comes with a mean little voice that says:
*"Who am I to do this?"* This chapter is your prepared answer, so you don't
have to argue with it fresh each time.

## Talk 1: You are not a fraud; you're a *bridge*

Sources of the impostor feeling, ranked:

1. You compare your *year one* to people's *year ten*. (Everyone does.)
2. Newness itself feels like danger. Your brain mistakes novelty for
   faking-ness.
3. You're actually the *opposite* of a fraud: you're the rare honest person —
   which is why you can *feel* the honesty so loudly.

Maya, week 14, about to publish her first piece:

> "I'm not a writer. I've had one thing published."
>
> Her pep response (written in advance, per the worksheet): *"A writer is
> someone who writes. I have written things. The rest is reps. Also — I'm
> the only writer in the room who has run a code alone at 3 a.m., and that
> authenticity is worth more than polish."*

## Talk 2: The "two chairs" trick

Sit in chair one, as the *doubt-voice*: list all the reasons you'll fail (all
of them; go wild). Then swap to chair two — as the *kind friend-listener* —
and answer each point once, briefly, out loud. This is not therapy; it's
clarity (and it takes four minutes; if it opens deep wells, see the note at
the bottom of this chapter).

## Talk 3: Your day job was never "just" anything

Nurses have run cardiac arrest. Teachers have held a room of 30 for 10
months. Warehouse leads have kept 40 tons of stuff flowing. The impostor
voice says *"you're starting over."* You're not. You're **re-deploying**,
with more experience than every 22-year-old starting the same journey —
which is a good weird feeling once you get used to it.

## Talk 4: Progress isn't a feeling; it's an artifact list

On days when you feel like a fake, the *feeling* is not the measurement.
The **artifacts** are:

- 1 worksheet filled in
- 1 skill inventoried
- 1 document rewritten
- 1 piece published
- 1 dollar of rung-2 income
- 1 kind reply from a stranger

Keep a plain text file. When the voice arrives, you don't debate it — you
open the file. That's your proof.

## Talk 5: The worst-case audit

Write down, honestly: "If this pivot fails completely in 3 years..."

- What do I lose? (Some weekends, some savings, maybe some comfort.)
- What do I carry back with me? (New skills, writing samples, a network,
  self-respect for having tried.)

Most people's honest worst case is *survivable and weirdly valuable*.
That's the actual reframe: pivots are rarely existentially dangerous; they're
just emotionally loud.

## Talk 6: Slow is normal (real timelines)

Real talk from real pivots: 18–36 months from the decision to a new-field
salary is *typical*, and the "first weird paid dollar" usually shows up at
month 3–9. If you're faster: lucky you, genuinely. Slow is not off-track.

## A gentle footnote (important)

These are pep talks, not treatment. Impostor feelings include a wide range of
normal human self-doubt — *and* sometimes, feeling chronically worthless,
panicky, or hopeless is a health issue worth real support. You know who you
are: **a crisis line (in the US: 988; elsewhere, your local number), a
therapist, a doctor, or a peer group.** This handbook is education, not
either kind of advice (medical, financial, or psychological).

### The one-sentence version

*"Who am I to do this?"* → **"Someone re-deploying real skills, in public,
with receipts — and receipts beat titles."**
